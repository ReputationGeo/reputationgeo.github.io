## What to do today if a screening may contain an error

If you are preparing for a job or tenancy screening, **background check errors** should be handled by documenting the incorrect information, identifying its source, and requesting a correction through the relevant channel.

- Write down the exact name and name variations connected with the issue.
- Record the URL, profile, directory entry, article, image, or other public asset where the information appears.
- Separate a request to correct information from a request to remove a source or improve the visibility of accurate information.
- Gather documents or other available evidence before contacting a platform or publisher.
- Keep a dated record of every submission, response, follow-up date, and status.

Do not begin by creating large amounts of new content or by assuming that every screening provider uses the same sources. The available brief does not establish one universal list of information that a background check company can see. The practical first step is to identify the specific inaccurate or outdated information and the place where it is published.

## What can a background check company see about you?

A background check company may encounter information connected with your name across online sources, but the exact sources and information available in a particular screening are not established here.

That distinction matters. A person preparing for screening should not assume that a public profile, search result, directory entry, article, image, or company page will automatically be included. They should also not assume that an item is harmless simply because it is old, difficult to find, or based on a name variation.

A responsible preparation process begins with an inventory of the online identity connected to you. The relevant categories in that inventory can include:

- Your full legal name and preferred public or professional name.
- Legitimate alternative name variations.
- Existing websites, profiles, directories, social platforms, publishing profiles, and professional pages.
- Articles and other published material associated with your name.
- Images associated with you.
- Information that is outdated, inaccurate, or connected to the wrong person.
- The search queries through which a person might find the information.

This inventory does not prove that a screening company has used any particular source. It gives you a factual basis for checking what is publicly associated with your identity and deciding what needs correction.

## How should you identify the exact error before contacting anyone?

Identify the precise statement, image, name association, or profile detail that is wrong, then preserve the page and record the source before requesting a change.

Create a simple issue record for each item. Include:

1. **The exact name or search query involved.** Record the full name, preferred name, and any legitimate variation that appears in the result.
2. **The source URL.** Save the complete address and the domain. If the information appears on a profile, directory, article, image page, or social account, record the specific page rather than only the home page.
3. **The content type.** Describe whether it is a profile, directory entry, article, image, account, listing, or another type of asset.
4. **The inaccurate or outdated statement.** Quote or describe only the relevant part. Avoid adding assumptions about what the publisher intended.
5. **The correct information.** Use facts supplied by you or independently verified. Do not replace one uncertain statement with another.
6. **Supporting documentation.** Note what documentation is available and where it is stored.
7. **The action required.** Mark the item for direct correction, a removal request where grounds exist, or monitoring and search-presence work where the source cannot be changed.
8. **The status.** Record whether the issue is planned, under review, submitted, published, indexed, needs an update, or otherwise unresolved.

Preserve the page details before contacting the publisher. A page may later change, become unavailable, or display different information. Keeping the original URL, content description, date checked, and available documentation creates a clearer record of what you asked to correct.

## What information should be in a correction request?

A correction request should identify the page, explain the specific factual problem, provide the accurate information, and attach or reference documentation that supports the request.

Keep the request narrow and factual. A useful submission should contain:

- Your name and the name variation connected with the page.
- The exact URL or profile identifier.
- A concise description of the inaccurate or outdated information.
- The corrected wording or the specific change requested.
- Documentation available to support the correction.
- The date of the request and a method for recording the response.

Do not submit credentials, employment history, education, licenses, awards, clients, relationships, locations, or achievements unless they have been supplied by you or independently verified. The identity-management process must never invent or embellish facts to make a profile appear stronger.

If the issue concerns mistaken identity, describe the mismatch precisely. For example, distinguish between a name variation that legitimately belongs to you and a page that associates your name with information belonging to another person. The request should focus on the factual connection that is wrong, not on speculation about the person who created the page.

Keep copies of the submitted text and all attachments. Add the submission date, response, follow-up date, and final status to your issue record.

## Which problems should be handled by direct correction or removal?

Direct action is appropriate when a source has a correction process, the requested change has a factual basis, and you can provide documentation supporting it.

Potentially removable or directly correctable URLs should be tracked separately from search-result suppression work. For each URL, record:

- URL and domain.
- Content type.
- Publication date, if available.
- Platform.
- Basis for the request.
- Documentation available.
- Submission date.
- Status.
- Follow-up date.
- Whether the content was actually changed or is still available.
- Whether it remains indexed or has become unavailable in search.

A request is not the same as a completed correction. Do not describe content as removed until it has been verified as unavailable at the source or appropriately deindexed. If a publisher updates a page, check the page itself rather than relying only on a search-result preview.

The same principle applies to inaccurate profiles and directories. A profile can be corrected only through the account owner, platform process, directory process, or another legitimate route available for that asset. Do not create a deceptive account or pretend to be an independent third party to influence the page.

## When is search-result suppression a different route?

Search-result suppression is a separate process for negative URLs that cannot be changed at the source and must be monitored independently from direct correction requests.

For each affected search query, record:

- The query.
- The negative URL.
- The starting position in the relevant search results.
- Positive assets appearing above it.
- New accurate assets created or strengthened.
- Movement over time.

This route does not change the underlying source. Its purpose is to build and maintain accurate, useful, and authoritative information that can become easier to find in relation to the target name or query. Suitable assets may include legitimate profiles, company pages, professional articles, directories, publishing profiles, and other content that you control or are authorized to manage.

Suppression should not be treated as a substitute for a correction request. If a source is inaccurate and has a legitimate correction process, pursue that process separately. If the source cannot be changed, maintain a separate suppression record rather than marking the source as corrected.

## Which routes do not work, and why?

Creating deceptive accounts, inventing credentials, publishing fabricated material, and treating a request as a completed change do not provide a legitimate correction route.

Several common shortcuts should be rejected:

- **Inventing professional facts:** Adding unverified education, employment, licenses, awards, clients, reviews, or accomplishments creates a new accuracy problem.
- **Pretending to be an independent third party:** A profile created to look independent when it is controlled by the subject is deceptive and should not be used.
- **Publishing identical biographies everywhere:** Repetition does not establish a clear identity and can make the digital footprint appear manufactured. Content should vary naturally while remaining consistent with verified facts.
- **Using a new page to erase an old source:** New content does not correct the original publisher’s page. Direct correction and search-presence work are separate activities.
- **Calling an unverified change complete:** A submitted request is not proof that a page was corrected, removed, or deindexed.
- **Creating content without checking the existing footprint:** New profiles or articles should not be produced before identifying existing URLs, inaccurate information, name variations, and official sources.

These routes fail because they do not address the factual source, do not create a reliable record, or introduce information that cannot be verified.

## How can you build one consistent identity before screening?

Create one canonical master entity record and use it as the single source of truth for every profile, article, directory entry, and official page.

The record should establish:

- Exact target name.
- Entity type, such as person, professional, executive, company, or brand.
- Primary location.
- Primary profession.
- Primary company.
- Main topics and supporting topics.
- Verified facts.
- Approved biographical facts.
- Prohibited or unverified claims.
- Official URLs.
- Target search queries.

This record prevents contradictions between assets. For example, one page should not state one number of years of experience while another states a different number unless both statements are factually compatible. It also helps keep a professional identity separate from unrelated information connected with a similar name.

When publishing or updating a page, confirm that the name, profession, company, location, services, areas of expertise, and official links match the canonical record. Use geographic information only where there is a legitimate connection. Use personal interests only when they are appropriate for public disclosure and approved for publication.

## What should you publish before a job or tenancy screening?

Publish or update only legitimate, useful assets that clarify your identity and support accurate information already in the master record.

A practical publishing sequence is:

1. Establish foundational profiles and canonical information.
2. Publish or update a professional biography.
3. Add useful expertise content that reflects subjects you legitimately understand.
4. Strengthen appropriate geographic, professional, or human-interest information where it is approved and factual.
5. Review the strongest assets and update them rather than creating large numbers of weak pages.

The aim is not to flood search results with pages about your name. Articles should provide genuine informational value, and profiles should belong on platforms where you or the business legitimately qualifies to have an account.

Where you control the relevant properties, connect them naturally. An official website may link to professional profiles, professional profiles may link to the official website, and an author profile may link to articles by that author. Avoid obvious circular linking schemes.

## How should you monitor the issue after submitting requests?

Monitor the source page and relevant search queries separately, recording each check and the next action.

A useful dashboard can contain:

- Client or subject name.
- Target query.
- Target country and language.
- Asset and platform.
- URL.
- Content type.
- Date created and date published.
- Whether the page is indexed.
- Search-result position where relevant.
- Status.
- Last checked date.
- Next action.

Use clear statuses such as planned, generated, review required, approved, published, indexed, ranking, needs update, failed, or removed. A status should describe what has actually happened, not what is intended.

Check whether the original page changed, whether the accurate profile remains available, and whether the information is still being associated with the wrong name variation. Keep direct-removal records separate from suppression records so that a lower search position is not mistaken for a source correction.

No fixed timetable should be assumed from the information available here. Record the dates of submission and follow-up, then update the status when a source or search result has been checked.

## FAQ

**Can I know exactly what a screening company will see?**

Not from a general checklist alone. The available information does not establish one universal set of sources used in every screening. Identify the public pages and name variations associated with you, then address specific inaccuracies through legitimate correction routes.

**Should I create new profiles immediately?**

No. First record the existing URLs, inaccurate information, name variations, official sources, and verified facts. Then create or update only legitimate profiles and content that fit the master entity record.

**Does improving a search result correct the original page?**

No. Search-result improvement and direct source correction are separate workflows. A source should not be described as corrected unless it has actually been updated, made unavailable, or appropriately deindexed and that status has been verified.

**What should I keep after submitting a correction request?**

Keep the URL, domain, content type, basis for the request, supporting documentation, submitted text, submission date, response, follow-up date, and final status.

**Can I add information that makes my profile look stronger?**

Only if the information has been supplied by you or independently verified. Do not invent credentials, employment, education, awards, clients, reviews, licenses, locations, relationships, or achievements.
