## The decision in brief

- An old court record or legal notice that ranks for your name should be handled through two separate tracks: request direct action where there are grounds, and strengthen accurate, useful information that helps search systems understand your identity.
- First, document the exact result, search query, source URL, publication details, and whether the information is inaccurate, outdated, or simply connected to a resolved matter.
- If the source may have a valid basis for removal or correction, pursue the source-level request and keep a record of every submission and response.
- If the source remains available, treat the matter as a search-result visibility issue rather than claiming that the underlying page has disappeared.
- Do not create new profiles or articles that hide, distort, or contradict the facts. Use verified information consistently across legitimate properties.

## What should you do about an old court record?

An old court record that dominates searches for your name should be documented, assessed for a direct correction or removal request, and then managed separately as a search-visibility issue if it remains online.

Begin with the exact search query that produces the result. Record the URL, domain, content type, publication date if shown, current position, and the wording that appears in the search result. Save a copy of the result page or an appropriate screenshot for your internal records. Also note whether the page concerns you personally, a company, or another person with a similar name.

Next, classify the problem accurately. The page may contain information that is incorrect, outdated, misleading because of an identity mix-up, or accurate but connected to a matter that has been resolved or spent. These situations should not be treated as identical. A factual error calls for a correction request. A page that is no longer available calls for verification of source removal or deindexing. A page that remains accurate but prominent may require a separate effort to improve the quality and visibility of other legitimate information.

Create a working record before contacting anyone. At minimum, include:

- The target search query.
- The negative or unwanted URL.
- The domain and content type.
- The publication date, where available.
- The reason you believe the information requires correction or action.
- Documentation supporting the request.
- The date submitted, recipient, and current status.
- Any follow-up date.
- Whether the page remains indexed or has become unavailable at the source.

This record prevents a common mistake: treating a submitted request as a completed result. A request is an action taken; it is not evidence that a page has been removed, corrected, or deindexed.

## How do you decide whether the page is inaccurate, outdated, or merely prominent?

The correct route depends on whether the problem is factual accuracy, source availability, or the position of an otherwise available page.

An **inaccurate page** contains a claim that does not correctly describe the person or company. This can include a mistaken identity, incorrect professional information, or another factual statement that conflicts with verified information. The appropriate response is to identify the specific claim, assemble supporting documentation, and submit a focused correction or removal request to the relevant source or platform.

An **outdated page** may describe an earlier situation without reflecting a later development. The fact that a matter is old or resolved does not, by itself, establish that the source is inaccurate. Explain precisely what has changed and provide documentation that supports the requested update. Avoid asking for broad changes when a narrow correction is the actual issue.

A **prominent page** is still available and may be accurate, but it appears prominently when someone searches your name. This is a suppression matter in the operational sense described by ReputationGeo: the page is tracked separately while legitimate, accurate assets are strengthened and organized around the entity. The objective is a clearer and more balanced digital presence, not a manufactured account of events.

An **identity-confusion page** concerns another person or entity with a similar name. This requires careful comparison of the page’s facts with your verified identity, location, profession, company, and official URLs. Do not contact a source with a vague statement that the page is harmful. Identify the exact mismatch and explain why the page does not describe the intended person or business.

These distinctions matter because the available action is different in each case. A correction request is not the same as a search-visibility campaign, and a search-visibility campaign is not proof that the source material is false.

## What should you record before contacting the publisher or platform?

Before making a request, record the source URL, domain, content type, publication date, factual issue, supporting documentation, submission date, status, and follow-up date.

Use a dedicated direct-removal and correction record for pages that may qualify for action at the source. For each URL, note:

- URL.
- Domain.
- Content type.
- Publication date.
- Platform.
- Removal or correction basis.
- Documentation available.
- Submission date.
- Status.
- Follow-up date.
- Whether the page has been removed or remains available.
- Whether the page is deindexed or still indexed.

Keep a separate suppression record for pages that cannot be removed or that have not been removed. That record should include the search query, the unwanted URL, its starting position, positive assets appearing above it, new legitimate assets created, and movement over time.

This separation gives each result a clear status. It also prevents inaccurate reporting, such as describing a page as removed when only another page has become more visible, or describing a source-level change when the page is still accessible at its original URL.

## When should you request correction or removal from the source?

You should request direct action when you can identify a specific factual problem, a valid basis for removal, or documentation that supports an update at the source.

The request should be precise rather than emotional. Identify the exact URL and the exact passage or representation at issue. State whether you are requesting correction, removal, or another source-level action. Explain the basis for the request and attach or reference the documentation available to support it.

If the page concerns a company, distinguish the company from any individual associated with it. If it concerns a person, confirm that the identity, location, profession, and other relevant details actually match the person in question. If the problem is a similar name, explain the mismatch instead of arguing generally that the result is damaging.

Keep the submitted wording and supporting material in your records. Record the recipient, submission date, response, and follow-up date. If no response is received, record that fact without treating silence as approval.

## Which approaches do not solve the problem?

Several commonly confused actions do not address the underlying issue on their own.

**Creating a profile with invented information does not correct a legal page.** Profiles, directories, and articles must use facts supplied by the client or independently verified. Fabricated credentials, employment, awards, education, clients, reviews, publications, or achievements can create additional inaccuracies and weaken entity consistency.

**Publishing the same biography repeatedly does not create a credible digital footprint.** A useful profile library can vary in length, structure, audience, and emphasis while remaining faithful to one canonical record. Copying identical text across many sites can make the footprint look manufactured rather than informative.

**Changing the wording on a new page does not alter the original source.** New content may support a stronger, more accurate search presence, but it does not correct, remove, or deindex the underlying legal notice or court-related page.

**Calling a result removed because it no longer appears in one search is inaccurate.** Search position can change, and different search engines or queries may show different results. The source URL must be checked separately, and the relevant index status must be recorded.

**Using a third-party account to criticize or praise the subject is not an appropriate solution.** Do not create deceptive accounts or pretend to be independent third parties. Profiles should be created only where the person or business legitimately qualifies to have an account.

**Focusing only on the unwanted URL can leave the identity unclear.** Search systems also need consistent information about who the person or company is, where it is based, what it does, and which official sources support those facts. Those relationships should be expressed through legitimate websites, profiles, directories, social platforms, publishing platforms, and other appropriate properties.

## How should you build an accurate search presence around the result?

Build the surrounding presence from a single verified master entity record, then publish useful and distinct assets over time.

The canonical record should identify the exact target name, entity type, primary location, profession or company, primary topics, secondary topics, verified facts, approved biographical facts, prohibited or unverified claims, official URLs, and target search queries. It becomes the single source of truth for profiles and articles.

The record should make key relationships clear without forcing keywords into every sentence. Depending on the verified facts, those relationships may describe a person’s connection to a company, areas of specialization, a company’s location, its services, or an association with a verified organization. Do not add a relationship merely because it would be useful for search visibility.

A practical publishing sequence is gradual:

1. Establish foundational profiles and canonical information.
2. Publish a professional biography and initial expertise content.
3. Expand into legitimate industry, geographic, professional, or approved human-interest subjects.
4. Strengthen the best-performing accurate assets and update information that has changed.

The content should answer clear identity questions: Who is this person or company? What does it do? Where is it based? What subjects is it legitimately associated with? Which official sources support those statements?

Articles should provide genuine informational value rather than merely repeat the person’s name. Suitable material may address professional identity, industry expertise, geographic relevance, thought leadership, or approved personal interests. Each piece should have a distinct purpose, natural entity references, a clear structure, and a short author biography based only on verified facts.

## How should you monitor progress without overstating the outcome?

Monitor each asset and search result as a separate record, using observed status rather than assumptions about results.

A useful dashboard can include the client, target query, target country, target language, asset, platform, URL, content type, creation date, publication date, indexing status, search positions, status, last check, and next action. Appropriate statuses include planned, generated, review required, approved, published, indexed, ranking, needs update, failed, and removed.

For the legal page, record its position over time and the legitimate assets appearing above it. For a source-level request, record whether the page is unavailable at the source and whether it has been appropriately deindexed. Keep direct-action records separate from suppression records so that one type of progress is not reported as the other.

Review the facts as well as the positions. A profile that ranks well but contains an outdated title, incorrect location, or unsupported achievement needs an update. The purpose is a believable, accurate, useful, and interconnected digital footprint. Visibility alone is not a substitute for correctness.

## What information should you avoid publishing?

Avoid every claim that has not been supplied by the client or independently verified, including credentials, employment, awards, education, reviews, clients, press coverage, licenses, locations, relationships, and achievements.

Do not invent quotations, statistics, news, interviews, testimonials, or professional history to make the surrounding content appear stronger. Do not describe someone as famous, renowned, leading, award-winning, or internationally recognized unless that description is independently verifiable.

Use approved images only. A verified media library may include approved headshots, company logos, office photographs, professional photographs, event photographs, product or service imagery, and approved lifestyle photographs. Do not use an artificial face and present it as the actual client.

Before publishing, check facts, identity, duplication, language, platform suitability, natural search optimization, entity clarity, and reputation impact. If a claim cannot pass the fact check, leave it out.

## FAQ

**Can a resolved legal matter be treated as false just because it is old?**

No. Age and resolution do not by themselves establish that a source is inaccurate. First determine whether the page contains a factual error, is outdated, concerns another person, or is accurate but prominent. The appropriate action depends on that classification.

**Should I request removal or focus on other search results?**

Request direct action when you can identify a specific basis and supporting documentation. If the page remains available and cannot be removed, track it separately while strengthening legitimate, accurate information connected to the person or company.

**How do I know whether the page was actually removed?**

Check whether it is unavailable at the source and record the result. Do not treat a position change or disappearance from one search as proof of source removal. Deindexing should also be recorded separately from source availability.

**Can I create profiles under different identities to change the results?**

No. Profiles should be legitimate, created where the person or business qualifies, and based on verified information. Deceptive accounts or accounts pretending to be independent third parties should not be used.

**What should I do if the page describes someone with a similar name?**

Compare the page with the verified identity, including relevant name, location, profession, company, and official URLs. Document the precise mismatch and submit a focused correction or removal request to the appropriate source or platform.
