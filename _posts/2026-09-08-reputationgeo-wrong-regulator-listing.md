## The decision in brief

- A wrong regulator listing should be corrected at the source, not hidden by publishing unrelated profiles.
- First, record the exact page, the incorrect statements, and the verified information that replaces them.
- Contact the regulator or professional body through its official correction, contact, or complaints route and keep proof of the request.
- Track the submission until the source page is corrected or the body gives a clear response; do not describe the information as corrected before checking the live page.
- If the listing cannot be corrected directly, keep source correction and search-result improvement as separate workstreams.

## What should you do about a wrong regulator listing?

A wrong regulator listing should be addressed by documenting the error, submitting verified supporting information to the responsible body, and monitoring the source page until its status is confirmed.

This order matters because the regulator or association controls the record. New biographies, social profiles, directory entries, and articles may strengthen the surrounding online presence, but they do not change the underlying listing. If the source continues to display incorrect information, searchers and automated systems may continue to rely on it.

The first task is therefore not content creation. It is source correction.

## What counts as an incorrect professional listing?

An incorrect listing is any published statement about the professional that conflicts with information supplied by the client or independently verified.

Examples may include an incorrect name variation, outdated position, wrong company association, inaccurate location, incorrect professional title, or a license-related detail that does not match the verified record. The same principle applies when a professional body displays a page belonging to the wrong person or combines details from two people.

Separate factual errors from matters of disagreement. A factual error can normally be described precisely: the page says one thing, while the verified record supports another. A disagreement about a decision, disciplinary outcome, professional status, or public description may require a different route and should not be presented as a simple editing request.

Do not broaden the request beyond what can be supported. If the only confirmed error is a company name, request correction of that company name rather than asking the body to rewrite the entire profile.

## What evidence should you collect before contacting the body?

Collect the exact source page, a record of each incorrect statement, and documentation supporting the replacement information before submitting a request.

Create a small correction file containing:

1. The full URL of the listing.
2. The name of the regulator or professional body.
3. The date the page was checked.
4. The text that is inaccurate, quoted exactly or described precisely.
5. The corrected wording you are asking the body to use.
6. Documentation available to support the correction.
7. The professional’s verified public name and relevant contact details.
8. Any related official URLs that help establish the correct identity.

Keep the original wording in your records. A page may change after you contact the publisher, and an unchanged copy can help show what the initial issue was. Record whether the page is still accessible, whether the incorrect statement remains, and whether the page appears to refer to the correct person.

Use only information that has been supplied by the professional or independently verified. Do not submit a résumé containing unverified awards, clients, employment, education, licenses, or achievements. A correction request supported by unsupported claims can create a second accuracy problem.

## How should the correction request be written?

Write a short, factual request that identifies the page, states the precise error, supplies the correction, and lists the supporting documentation.

A practical structure is:

**Subject:** Request to correct information on professional listing

**Page:** [exact listing URL]

**Incorrect information:** [the statement currently shown]

**Correct information:** [the verified replacement]

**Reason for request:** [brief explanation of the factual discrepancy]

**Supporting documentation:** [documents or official sources available]

**Requested action:** Please review the listing and update the inaccurate information if your records confirm the correction.

**Follow-up record:** [sender, date submitted, and any reference number]

Keep the tone neutral. The objective is to make the record accurate, not to pressure the publisher into removing information that is properly supported. Avoid accusations, threats, emotional language, and unrelated complaints. Do not claim that a listing is defamatory, unlawful, or removable unless that position has been independently established and is relevant to the route being used.

Ask for a review of the specific factual issue. If the body has a correction form, use that form. If it provides a contact or complaints channel, send the request there. The research brief does not establish a universal form, response period, or escalation process, so do not assume that every regulator follows the same procedure.

## Which route should you use: correction, removal, or search-result work?

Use a correction route when the source is legitimate but contains inaccurate information; consider a removal request only where there are documented grounds; use search-result work separately when the source cannot be changed.

These routes solve different problems:

### Source correction

This is the appropriate starting point when the professional body or regulator has a legitimate listing and one or more factual details are wrong. The request goes to the organization responsible for the page. The desired outcome is an accurate source record.

### Direct removal request

Removal is a separate workflow. The relevant record should include the URL, domain, content type, publication date if available, platform, removal basis, documentation, submission date, current status, follow-up date, and whether the page has actually become unavailable or been deindexed.

A removal request should not be described as successful merely because it was submitted. The page should be checked at the source, and any relevant search visibility status should be recorded independently.

### Search-result improvement

If a legitimate source cannot be changed, additional accurate assets may help create a stronger, more consistent search presence over time. This can include an official website, legitimate professional profiles, appropriate directories, and useful expertise content. These assets must be based on verified facts and should not pretend to be independent third-party commentary.

Search-result work does not correct the source record. It also should not be used to conceal a factually accurate regulatory entry. Its proper role is to make the professional’s broader identity clearer and to strengthen accurate information that already exists.

## Which approaches usually fail, and why?

Creating unrelated pages, repeating the same biography, or asking search platforms to fix a source-controlled record will not correct the underlying listing.

Several common approaches are confused with source correction:

**Publishing many new profiles.** New profiles may repeat the correct information, but they do not give the regulator or association a reason to update its own page. Large volumes of weak or duplicated pages can also make the online presence look manufactured.

**Using a biography as evidence.** A professional biography explains identity and expertise; it is not automatically proof of licensing, employment, education, or association membership. Those claims need supporting records.

**Requesting search deindexing as the first step.** Deindexing affects discoverability in a search service, not necessarily the page at its source. If the goal is to correct an official listing, contact the responsible body first.

**Creating a supposedly independent article.** A company-controlled article should not be presented as independent reporting, a neutral review, or a third-party assessment. It may provide useful context, but its ownership should be clear.

**Using a different name or inconsistent facts.** Altering the professional name, location, employer, or credentials across profiles can create identity confusion. One canonical record should control every public asset.

**Sending an unsupported demand.** A request that supplies no precise error or evidence is difficult to evaluate. A clear correction request is more useful than a general statement that the page is damaging.

## How do you maintain one accurate identity across platforms?

Maintain a canonical master entity record and use it as the single source of truth for every profile, article, directory entry, and correction request.

The record should identify:

- The exact target name and legitimate name variations.
- Whether the entity is a person, company, executive, professional, or brand.
- The verified primary location.
- The verified profession and company association.
- Areas of expertise and services.
- Education, certifications, licenses, awards, associations, publications, and speaking engagements only when supplied or independently verified.
- Official websites and existing profile URLs.
- The exact search query being addressed.
- Incorrect information requiring correction.
- Prohibited or unverified claims.

This prevents one profile from stating one experience period while another uses a conflicting figure. It also helps distinguish facts that are approved for public use from facts that have not been verified or should not be published.

Before publishing an update, compare it with the master record. Check the name, professional identity, location, company, credentials, dates, and relationships. If the regulator has corrected one detail, update controlled profiles where appropriate so the wider presence remains consistent.

## How should you track the request after submission?

Track the request as a separate correction case with its own evidence, status, and follow-up record.

A useful case record includes:

- Source URL.
- Organization responsible for the listing.
- Incorrect field or statement.
- Proposed correction.
- Supporting documentation available.
- Submission date.
- Submission method.
- Reference number, if provided.
- Response received.
- Follow-up date.
- Current page status.
- Date the live page was last checked.

Use clear statuses such as planned, submitted, review required, responded, corrected, needs follow-up, or unable to verify. Do not mark the matter corrected based only on an email response. Open the live page and confirm that the relevant text has changed.

If the body declines the request, keep the response and record the stated reason. The next step depends on the reason given. A missing document may call for a better-supported submission. A disagreement over a substantive decision may belong in a formal review or complaints process rather than an ordinary profile-edit request. The correct route should follow the organization’s own published process.

## How can you publish supporting information without creating new problems?

Publish only legitimate, useful assets that clarify the professional’s identity and expertise without repeating unsupported claims or pretending to be independent sources.

An official website can link to legitimate professional profiles. Author pages can connect to articles that the professional actually wrote. Company profiles can link to the official company website where the relationship is genuine. These links should be natural and should not form an obvious circular network designed only to manipulate visibility.

Content should answer basic identity questions clearly: who the professional is, what they do, where they operate, what subjects they understand, and which organizations they are legitimately associated with. It should not imply awards, clients, credentials, employment, locations, or accomplishments that have not been verified.

Use different biographies for genuinely different platforms rather than copying one paragraph everywhere. A professional network may need a concise factual description, while a publishing profile may focus on legitimate topics. Variation should change the structure and emphasis, not the facts.

## What should you check before closing the case?

Close the case only after the source page, supporting assets, and records have been checked for accuracy and consistency.

Use this final checklist:

- The exact source page was checked again.
- The incorrect statement was compared with the submitted correction.
- The live page now reflects the verified information, or the unresolved status is recorded accurately.
- The correction response and supporting documentation are stored.
- Controlled profiles use the same verified name, profession, company, location, and credentials.
- No new article or profile makes an unsupported claim.
- Direct correction and search-result work remain separate in the records.
- Any remaining inaccurate page is tracked with its current position and follow-up action.
- The next review date is recorded.

If the source remains unchanged, describe it as unresolved or pending rather than corrected. Accuracy in the case record matters because future content and decisions will depend on it.

## FAQ

**Can I fix the listing by creating a new professional profile?**

No. A new legitimate profile may clarify the professional’s identity elsewhere, but it does not change a regulator’s or association’s source record. Contact the organization responsible for the listing for a correction.

**Should I request removal when only one detail is wrong?**

Usually, a correction request is the more precise route when the listing is legitimate and only a factual detail is inaccurate. Removal is a separate workflow and should be considered only where there are documented grounds.

**What if the organization does not update the page?**

Record the response, keep the documentation, and follow the organization’s available review or complaints process where appropriate. Separately, accurate official profiles and useful content may support a clearer online presence, but they do not change the unchanged source page.

**How do I know the correction is complete?**

Check the live listing and confirm that the inaccurate information has changed. Do not rely only on a submitted form, an email, or a promise of review.
